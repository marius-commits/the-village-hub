Hourly, half day or full day — which to book

Most people over-book or under-book, and both cost money.

  • By the hour (1–2 hours) — a board meeting with a tight agenda, an interview panel, a client pitch, a signing. If your agenda has times against each item, book by the hour.
  • Half day (4 hours) — a strategy session, a quarterly review, an interview morning with four or five candidates back to back. The usual windows are 08:00–12:00 and 13:00–17:00.
  • Full day (8 hours) — training, workshops, planning days, anything with breakout work. A full day is worth it the moment you need the room set up before delegates arrive and left standing over lunch.

The common mistake is booking two half days on consecutive dates when a single full day would have done. You pay for the room twice, you set up twice, and you lose the momentum that comes from keeping the flipcharts on the wall over the lunch break. The reverse mistake is booking a full day for a two-hour decision, which is just money left on a table you did not need.

One more thing worth asking: does the clock start when you arrive or when the meeting starts? If you need forty-five minutes to set up a workshop, that time is real and somebody is paying for it.

The five extras that are usually not in the quote

A quoted room rate is rarely the number that lands on the invoice. These five are where the gap comes from.

  • Parking. The single biggest hidden cost of a town-centre meeting. Ten delegates paying for a parkade for six hours is real money, and it comes out of their pockets or yours. Ask specifically: is parking free, on site, and enough for everyone?
  • Audio-visual. Some venues quote the room and then price the screen, the clicker and the conferencing camera as line items. Ask what is in the room already and what you would be charged for.
  • Catering minimums. Watch for a minimum spend per head, or a rule that you must use the in-house caterer. Neither is unreasonable, but both change your budget.
  • After-hours and weekend loading. Many venues carry a surcharge outside standard business hours. If your session runs to 18:30, confirm what that last half hour costs.
  • Setup and layout changes. Moving from boardroom to classroom layout is sometimes free and sometimes a fee. Confirm before the day, not on it.

What actually drives the price

Four things move a meeting room rate in Stellenbosch, and it is worth knowing which you are paying for.

  • Capacity. Seats are the crudest driver. A 12-seater costs more than a 10-seater because the room is bigger and harder to fill.
  • Fit-out and A/V. A room with a proper 4K display, conferencing camera and acoustic treatment prices above a room with a table and a TV on a trolley. If you are running a hybrid meeting with people dialling in, this is the part worth paying for.
  • Location. Central Stellenbosch carries a premium for walkability — and passes the parking problem to you. Out-of-centre venues trade a short drive for free parking and calm.
  • Hospitality. Whether coffee, water and catering are handled for you or left to you. This is the difference between hosting a meeting and running a venue on the day.

A venue that is cheap on all four is cheap for a reason. A venue that is expensive on all four may be selling you a wine-estate view you did not need for a Tuesday board meeting. Decide which two matter for this particular session and pay for those.

How to get an accurate quote in one message

Most quote delays come from missing information, not slow venues. Send these six things and you should get a firm number straight back:

  1. Date and times, including setup and pack-down.
  2. Headcount, and how many are dialling in.
  3. Layout — boardroom, U-shape, classroom or pods.
  4. Catering — arrival coffee, mid-morning, lunch, dietary requirements.
  5. A/V — screen, camera, anything you are bringing yourself.
  6. Recurrence — is this a one-off or the first of several? Say so; it changes the rate.

That last point is the one most people skip and it is the one that saves the most. A venue quotes a one-off differently from a standing monthly booking.

One thing worth knowing about hiring in Stellenbosch

Stellenbosch has an unusual meeting-venue market. It carries a genuine business district, a university, and a ring of wine estates that will all happily host your strategy day. That breadth is a gift when you want an occasion and a trap when you want a working meeting.

The estates price for the setting. The CBD prices for the address and hands you the parking problem. The useful middle — a professional room, free parking, real Wi-Fi, coffee that arrives without you chasing it, and a restaurant downstairs — is smaller than it should be. That middle is where we have deliberately put The Village Hub's two boardrooms, about ten minutes from the centre off the R304.

Whichever way you go, the rule holds: price the day, not the room. The room rate is often the smallest number in the total.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a half-day and a full-day booking?

A half day is four hours, usually 08:00–12:00 or 13:00–17:00, and suits board meetings, strategy sessions and interview panels. A full day is eight hours and suits training and workshops where the room needs to be set up early and left standing over lunch. A full day also keeps your material on the walls through the break, which matters more than people expect.

What extras are usually left out of a quote?

Parking, A/V hire, catering minimums, after-hours surcharges and layout-change fees. Ask about all five before confirming.

Is it cheaper to meet outside the Stellenbosch CBD?

The hourly rate is often similar. The total cost of the day usually is not, because a town-centre booking adds paid parking per delegate and the time cost of finding a bay. A venue just outside the centre with free on-site parking removes both.

Yes. Recurring and multi-day corporate bookings are quoted as a block rather than by the hour, and members book at member rates. Send the dates and headcount and we will quote the block.
